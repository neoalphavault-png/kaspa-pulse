Kaspa data sources, compared. Which one to open for which question, and why two of them can show you a different number for the same thing.

There is no single place that answers everything about Kaspa, and the sites that come closest are each built around a different idea of what a transaction is. That is the whole reason their numbers disagree. Here is what each one is genuinely best at, and how to read all of them without getting fooled.
The short answer

Use Kaspalytics for on-chain history and supply structure, an explorer for a single transaction or address, and a market site for price. Kaspalytics is the deepest free chart set on Kaspa, covering supply aging, exchange holdings, address buckets and covenant activity. For one specific transaction, address or block, use explorer.kaspa.org or the open REST API behind it. For KRC-20 tokens use kas.fyi. For price, volume and exchange data, any market site will do. Nobody is wrong when the numbers differ, they are usually answering different questions.

before you trust any number on any of them
3 questions
Ask these three of any chart on any site, including ours. Nearly every argument about Kaspa numbers dissolves once all three are answered.
What counts?
does a transaction mean every one in every block, or each one once after the DAG has accepted it. this single choice can move a number by 5x
Over what window?
a live per-second ticker and a 24 hour average of the same thing are different numbers, and neither is a mistake
How fresh?
network views refresh in seconds, supply and address aging usually once a day. a flat line can just mean today has not been written yet

Two things are worth saying plainly. First, almost all of this is free and made by people in this community for nothing, which is not normal for a chain this size. Second, none of these tools is trying to mislead anyone. When two of them disagree, the cause is nearly always a definition, not an error.

Why two sites show a different transactions per second

This is the one that catches people out most often, so it is worth walking through with the real numbers rather than in the abstract. In August 2026 a live counter on one site was showing somewhere around 11 transactions per second while our own dashboard was publishing 2.34 for the same week. Both figures are defensible. They count different things.

What we count, exactly

We take the accepted transactions for a full day, standard transactions plus coinbase transactions, and divide by the 86,400 seconds in that day. Accepted matters: Kaspa produces around ten blocks a second in parallel, so the same transaction can appear in more than one block before the DAG settles which of them counts. Counting accepted transactions counts each one once, after that has been decided. Dividing by a whole day means we publish an average across quiet nights and busy afternoons alike, not a moment.

What a live ticker counts

A real-time counter is built to show flow, so it counts what is streaming past right now, which includes the coinbase transaction that every one of those ten blocks a second carries with it. We cannot see anyone else's code and we do not claim to know exactly how another site counts. What we can say is that a per-second live reading and a per-day accepted average are different measurements, and that the gap between them on Kaspa is structural, not a bug in either.

The practical rule: a transactions per second figure means nothing without the word before it. Ask whether it is accepted or raw, whether coinbase transactions are included, and whether it is an average or a live reading. Every number on our dashboard carries its definition for exactly this reason.

Where our own numbers come from

We are a source on this list, so the same standard applies to us. The weekly figures on the dashboard come from Kaspalytics for supply aging, exchange holdings, active addresses and accepted transaction counts, from api.kaspa.org where we count something ourselves transaction by transaction, and from DeFiLlama for layer 2 value. Hash rate and difficulty are cross-checked against more than one source before they go out.

Where we cannot verify a number, we print a dash instead of a guess. That is why the dashboard occasionally shows blanks while other sites show a figure for the same tile. A dash means we could not stand behind it that week, and we would rather be visibly incomplete than quietly wrong. When we do make a claim about what a wallet did, we counted every transaction ourselves and we report the movement, never the motive.
